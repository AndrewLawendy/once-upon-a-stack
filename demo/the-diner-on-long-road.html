A Slice, Now

One slice, please

Every day at lunch, Rosie (your app) walks into the little diner at the end of Long Road and asks for one thing: a slice of pie.

She has a short lunch break. If the pie takes too long, she leaves hungry.

That's the whole story, really. Someone wants something, and wants it now.

Every app is like this. Each time a person opens a page, the app needs some data to show: a name, a price, a list of messages. That data is the pie.

For grown-ups

Who she is
Inferred Rosie (your app) stands for the code that serves a request, such as a web server. Verified The memcache paper describes the read path the same way: "When a web server needs data, it first requests the value from memcache by providing a string key", and on a miss reads the database (s2).
Why "now" matters
Verified "A single user web request can often result in hundreds of individual memcache get requests." Every small delay is multiplied by how many pieces of data one page needs.

Nishtala et al., Scaling Memcache at Facebook, NSDI 2013, s2 and s3.1

A Slice, Now

The pies live at the farm

The diner has no oven. Every pie in the county is baked at one farm, far down Long Road, by the Baker (the database).

Why only one place? Because everyone must get the same pie. If every diner baked its own, each would follow its own recipe, and nobody could say which apple pie is the real one.

So the farm keeps the one true recipe book. When a recipe changes, it changes there, and only there.

Rosie (your app) orders her slice from the farm.

For grown-ups

The source of truth
The Baker (the database) is the system of record: durable storage that every app instance reads and writes, so they all agree. Inferred from the standard look-aside design, where writes go to the database and the cache is only a copy.
Writes go to the farm
Verified At Facebook, a web server that changes data "issues SQL statements to the database and then sends a delete request to memcache". The database is updated; the cache is only told to forget.

Nishtala et al., NSDI 2013, s2 (Figure 1)

  1. Rosie (your app) orders a slice. A truck sets off down Long Road.
  2. The Baker (the database) bakes, and the truck drives all the way back.
  3. The next customer wants the same apple pie. Another truck, another trip.
  4. At lunchtime everyone orders at once, and the Baker (the database) is buried in identical orders.

A Slice, Now

Every slice takes the long road

The first slice is lovely. It's also slow: the order goes all the way to the farm, and the slice comes all the way back. Step through it.

Then the next person wants the same apple pie, and the whole trip happens again. At lunch, the Baker (the database) spends all day baking the same pie over and over, and every order waits behind the others.

What people see: pages that load slowly, then slower at the busiest hour, and finally time out.

For grown-ups

Why a database trip is the slow path
Verified Facebook uses memcache "to reduce the frequency of fetching data along more expensive paths such as database queries", and "to lighten the read load on our databases".
The same pie, over and over
Verified Some keys are very hot: "a single key can account for 20% of a server's requests". Inferred A few popular things can dominate a server's work.
Why it gets worse at peak
Inferred A database has a fixed number of connections and disk and CPU budget. Past that, requests queue, so every request waits for the ones ahead of it.

Nishtala et al., NSDI 2013, s2, s3.2, s3.3

A Slice, Now

A pie case on the counter

Here's the trick. The farm bakes whole pies, so why carry back one slice and throw away the rest?

Meet Della (the cache), a glass pie case right on the counter. When a whole pie comes back from the farm, the rest of it goes into her.

Now the next person who wants apple gets a slice from the case in seconds. No truck, no trip, and the Baker (the database) can bake something else.

That's the whole idea of a cache: a copy of what people keep asking for, near the people asking.

For grown-ups

What the case is
Verified memcached, the open-source cache server Facebook builds on, is "an in-memory hash table running on a single server" (s3). Memory is much faster than a database query that may touch disk and do work.
Why it helps the farm, not just for the app
Verified The design goal is to shield the database: "We are willing to expose slightly stale data in exchange for insulating a backend storage service from excessive load." That trade, speed and protection for some staleness, comes back in chapter two.

Nishtala et al., NSDI 2013, s2, s3. The "3 sec" in the scene is story scale: a real cache hit takes well under a millisecond (the paper reports a median of 333 microseconds).

  1. Rosie (your app) asks for a pie by name. The name is the key.
  2. It's there: a hit. A slice in seconds.
  3. Plum? Not in the case: a miss.
  4. Rosie (your app) orders plum from the farm herself, then puts the pie in the case for next time.

A Slice, Now

Wait, how does the case know?

Della (the cache) doesn't understand pies. She keeps labels and what's under them. Ask for "apple pie" and she either has a pie under that label, or she doesn't.

The surprise is step 4: on a miss, the case doesn't drive to the farm. Rosie (your app) does. She orders from the Baker (the database), gets her slice, and puts the rest in the case herself.

And that's the goal met. Whenever her pie is already in the case, she's eating in seconds.

For grown-ups

Key and value
Verified The web server "first requests the value from memcache by providing a string key". The pie's name is the key; the pie is the value.
Who fills the cache
Verified On a miss, the web server "retrieves the data from the database or other backend service and populates the cache with the key-value pair". This is a "demand-filled look-aside cache": the app looks aside to the cache, and fills it only when asked. memcached itself "provides no server-to-server coordination" (s3).
Other designs
Some caches sit in front and fetch on a miss themselves (read-through), such as HTTP proxies and CDNs. This book follows look-aside, the common pattern with Redis and memcached. Inferred

Nishtala et al., NSDI 2013, s2

Today's pie, please

Imagine if

Speed has a price. On Thursday the farm changes its apple recipe, and the price goes up. But the pie in Della (the cache) was put there on Monday, and nobody told her.

If pies stay in the case forever, Rosie (your app) keeps getting Monday's pie and Monday's price. Drag across the picture.

So now she wants something new: not just a fast pie, but today's pie.

What people see: "I changed my name an hour ago and the site still shows the old one."

For grown-ups

Stale, but the cache can't tell
Verified In HTTP caching, "fresh" is a clock rule: a response is fresh while its age hasn't passed its freshness lifetime. Nothing checks it against the source. Inferred So a copy can be fresh by the clock and wrong in fact.
Keep forever
Inferred With no expiry and no invalidation, a copy leaves the cache only if it's evicted. Staleness has no upper limit.

RFC 9111 s4.2

The Right Pie

A date sticker on every pie

First fix: every pie that goes into Della (the cache) gets a sticker, "sell within ten minutes". When it runs out, the pie is thrown away, and the next order goes back to the farm for a fresh one.

Now a wrong pie can't last forever. But how long should the sticker be? Drag across.

Short: pies are nearly always right, but the trucks never stop, and the Baker (the database) is busy again. Long: hardly any trips, and Rosie (your app) gets old pie for days.

For grown-ups

The sticker is the TTL
Verified HTTP: Cache-Control: max-age=600 gives a freshness lifetime in seconds; the cache sends Age, its estimate of how long ago the response was generated or last successfully validated. Redis: keys can carry an expiration (TTL), for example SET k v EX 600.
Signs your sticker is wrong
Verified Redis's docs say a high expired_keys count can mean "a TTL that is too low". Hit ratio is keyspace_hits / (keyspace_hits + keyspace_misses).
The trade-off
Inferred Without invalidation, the worst-case staleness is about one TTL. Halving the TTL roughly doubles the refills for a steadily read key.

RFC 9111 s4.2, s5.1, s5.2.2.1; redis.io key eviction docs (INFO section), fetched 2026-11-01

  1. Rosie (your app) misses, orders apple. The Baker (the database) hands over the old pie, and a slow truck sets off.
  2. Then the Baker (the database) changes the recipe and phones: "throw out the apple!"
  3. The case is empty. Nothing to throw out.
  4. The slow truck finally arrives, and the old pie goes into the case. It stays there until its sticker runs out.

The Right Pie

A phone call from the farm

Better fix: whenever the recipe changes, the Baker (the database) phones Della (the cache): "throw out the apple!" The next order then fetches the new pie. Most changes now show up at once.

Why "throw out", not "here's the new pie"? Throwing out twice is harmless.

But even throwing out has a gap. Step through it: a slow truck can arrive after the call, and put the old pie back.

What people see: a change that "didn't take", for one item, until the sticker runs out.

For grown-ups

Delete on write
Verified At Facebook, a write updates the database, then "sends a delete request to memcache that invalidates any stale data". The delete comes from the web server, or from mcsqueal, a daemon on the database that reads its commit log (s4.1). "We choose to delete cached data instead of updating it because deletes are idempotent." HTTP caches invalidate a URL after a successful PUT, POST or DELETE passes through them (RFC 9111 s4.4).
The stale set
Verified "A stale set occurs when a web server sets a value in memcache that does not reflect the latest value that should be cached. This can occur when concurrent updates to memcache get reordered." Inferred The four steps on this page are one such reordering: miss, read old value, write and delete, then set old value.
Other gaps
Inferred A delete can be lost if the cache is down; a write through another path (an admin script) may never delete; browser and CDN copies don't hear the call. The TTL is the backstop for all of them.

Nishtala et al., NSDI 2013, s2, s3.2.1; RFC 9111 s4.4

The Right Pie

Good enough, and honest about it

The phone call makes most changes show up at once. The sticker makes sure that even a pie that slips through can't last longer than its date.

Big diners close the last gap with a claim ticket. On a miss, Della (the cache) hands out a numbered ticket, and the delivery must show it. The phone call cancels the ticket, so the slow truck's old pie, with its dead ticket, is turned away.

And Rosie (your app) gets today's pie, almost always. A cache never promises "always". It promises fast, and fresh enough.

For grown-ups

Leases
Verified "A memcached instance gives a lease to a client to set data back into the cache when that client experiences a cache miss. The lease is a 64-bit token bound to the specific key the client originally requested." The set carries the token, and "verification can fail if memcached has invalidated the lease token due to receiving a delete request for that item".
The deal
Verified "We are willing to expose slightly stale data in exchange for insulating a backend storage service from excessive load."
Not everywhere
Inferred Leases need a cache and client that support them. Stock Redis has none; check what your memcached version and client offer. Most apps live with TTL plus delete.

Nishtala et al., NSDI 2013, s2, s3.2.1

Room in the Case

Keep my favourite

Della (the cache) is fast because she's small and close. Small means she fills up.

On a busy day the case is full before any sticker runs out. A plum pie arrives, and there's nowhere to put it.

Rosie (your app) has a favourite, and she wants it in the case when she comes in. For that, something has to go. But what?

What people see, if nothing goes: saving fails with OOM command not allowed when used memory > 'maxmemory'.

For grown-ups

The size of the case
Verified Redis's limit is maxmemory (default 0, no limit on 64-bit). "Whenever a client runs a new command that adds more data to the cache, Redis checks the memory usage. If it is greater than the limit, Redis evicts keys according to the chosen eviction policy until the total memory used is back below the limit."
The default is: don't throw anything out
Verified maxmemory-policy defaults to noeviction. At the limit, writes are rejected with the error above, and "commands that only read existing data still work as normal". Redis 7.4.0, src/config.c and src/server.c (shared.oomerr).
Why not keep everything
Inferred RAM is finite and costly. With no limit, the process grows until the operating system kills it.

redis.io key eviction docs, fetched 2026-11-01; Redis 7.4.0 src/config.c, src/server.c

Room in the Case

Throw one out at random?

Imagine if

The quickest rule: when the case is full, close your eyes and take out any pie.

It's cheap, and blind. The pie that goes might be one nobody wanted all week, or the apple pie that is Rosie (your app)'s favourite.

Throw out the popular pie, and the next twenty customers all miss: twenty trips down Long Road.

What people see: the cache "works" but pages are slow, because the busiest data keeps vanishing.

For grown-ups

Random eviction is real
Verified Redis offers allkeys-random and volatile-random. Its docs suggest allkeys-random "when you expect all keys to be accessed with roughly equal frequency".
Why it hurts
Inferred Real traffic is uneven, so a random victim is usually cold but sometimes very hot; each hot-key eviction costs one miss per reader until it's refilled.

redis.io key eviction docs, fetched 2026-11-01

  1. The case is full. Eddie (the eviction policy) the busboy comes over.
  2. He picks five pies at random and reads when each was last asked for.
  3. Rhubarb: three days. Nobody wants rhubarb.
  4. Out goes rhubarb. Apple, asked for a minute ago, stays.

Room in the Case

The busboy clears the oldest plate

Meet Eddie (the eviction policy), the busboy. His rule: when there's no room, take away the pie that nobody has asked for the longest. A pie asked for recently will probably be asked for again.

Checking every pie would take him all day, so he cheats a little. He picks a handful at random and clears the stalest of those. It's nearly as good, and much quicker.

So Rosie (your app)'s favourite stays, because she keeps asking for it. Goal met.

For grown-ups

LRU, approximately
Verified Redis's allkeys-lru "samples a small number of keys at random and then evicts the ones with the longest time since last access". maxmemory-samples defaults to 5; at 10 it closely approximates true LRU, at more CPU cost. Since Redis 3.0 it also keeps a pool of good candidates (EVPOOL_SIZE 16 in src/evict.c).
Why not exact LRU
Verified "The reason Redis does not use a true LRU implementation is because it costs more memory."
When LRU is wrong
Verified Redis 4.0 added LFU (allkeys-lfu), which keeps a small frequency counter per key, for when "used recently" isn't the same as "used often". Inferred One big scan of cold keys can push hot keys out under LRU.

redis.io key eviction docs, fetched 2026-11-01; Redis 7.4.0 src/evict.c, src/config.c

Noon

Pie, even at noon

Everything works. Pies are fast, fresh enough, and the favourites stay. Rosie (your app) has one last wish: that it keeps working at the busiest moment of the day.

Look at the apple pie in Della (the cache). Its sticker says "sell by 12:00". At noon, the whole town walks in, and most of them want apple.

The sticker runs out. The pie goes. Two hundred hungry people are standing at the counter, and the case is empty.

For grown-ups

Hot keys
Verified At Facebook "a single key can account for 20% of a server's requests". When such a key expires, many requests miss at the same moment.
How many at once
Verified "If the cache item is accessed 10 times per second, and the recomputation of the item takes 3 seconds, then 30 requests will recompute the item." Busier keys and slower rebuilds make it worse.

Nishtala et al., NSDI 2013, s3.3; Vattani et al., PVLDB 8(8) 2015, s1

  1. The case is empty, and two hundred people want apple.
  2. Every one of them misses, and every one sends an order to the farm.
  3. Two hundred orders for the same pie jam the road.
  4. The Baker (the database) slows down, so each pie takes longer, so more people pile up. It feeds itself.

Noon

Two hundred orders at once

Every miss sends its own order. That rule was fine for one person. At noon it turns two hundred hungry people into the Rush (a cache stampede).

He comes back every time a busy pie's sticker runs out at a busy hour. Step through it.

The cruel part: the Baker (the database) was protected only while Della (the cache) had the pie. The moment she doesn't, he gets the whole town at once.

What people see: the site slows to a crawl or falls over, at exactly its busiest moment, for no obvious reason.

For grown-ups

Cache stampede
Verified "A cache stampede is often referred to as a cascading failure because several concurrent re-computations will increase the time of each individual recomputation by bogging down the system, in turn causing even more requests to be part of the stampede." Also called dog-piling or a cache miss storm.
Measured
Verified On a set of Facebook keys prone to this, without protection "all of the cache misses resulted in a peak database query rate of 17K/s". Facebook's paper calls its version a thundering herd, driven by frequent deletes rather than expiry.
Why a villain
It recurs on every hot-key expiry, and it grows itself: that's why it's drawn as a character, not weather.

Vattani et al., PVLDB 8(8) 2015, s1; Nishtala et al., NSDI 2013, s3.2.1

Noon

Just make the sticker longer?

Imagine if

If the pie runs out at noon, put a longer sticker on it. Sell by six!

At six, the dinner crowd walks in, the sticker runs out, and the Rush (a cache stampede) is back. A longer sticker only moves him. It never sends him away.

And it costs something: the longer the sticker, the older the pie, which is exactly what chapter two was about.

Rosie (your app) needs something that stops the two hundred orders, not just one that delays them.

For grown-ups

TTL doesn't fix it
Inferred A stampede happens at every expiry of a hot key. A longer TTL lowers how often, not how hard, and widens the staleness window.
Other dead ends
Verified The VLDB paper lists a background recompute job ("would even regenerate cache items that were never requested") and a lock, which needs its own expiry and is "not fault-tolerant: if the request acquiring the lock fails while re-generating the item, no item or a stale item will be served until the lock expires".

Vattani et al., PVLDB 8(8) 2015, s1

  1. Noon. Apple has run out, and the orders start coming.
  2. The first apple order goes to the farm, as usual.
  3. Every other apple order is pinned on Tess (request coalescing) to wait. No more trucks.
  4. One pie comes back, and everyone on the spike gets a slice of it.

Noon

One order per pie

Meet Tess (request coalescing), the ticket spike by the till. Her rule is short: one order per pie.

The first person to miss apple sends the order. Everyone after that who wants apple gets their ticket pinned on her, and waits. When the pie comes back, they all get slices from it.

Two hundred misses, one truck. The Baker (the database) bakes one pie instead of two hundred, and the Rush (a cache stampede) has nothing to push.

For grown-ups

singleflight
Verified Go's golang.org/x/sync/singleflight (v0.24.0): Do "executes and returns the results of the given function, making sure that only one execution is in-flight for a given key at a time. If a duplicate comes in, the duplicate caller waits for the original to complete and receives the same results."
One spike per diner
Inferred A singleflight.Group lives in one process's memory, so 100 app servers still send up to 100 queries. Verified Facebook limits this in the cache instead: each memcached hands out a lease token for a key "only once every 10 seconds per key", and other clients are told "to wait a short amount of time" and retry, rather than sharing the first caller's result. On herd-prone keys, peak database load fell from 17K/s to 1.3K/s.
When the first order fails
Inferred Everyone waiting shares its result, including its error, and a hung fetch holds everyone. Use a timeout.

pkg.go.dev/golang.org/x/sync/singleflight (v0.24.0); Nishtala et al., NSDI 2013, s3.2.1

Noon

A slice while you wait

Tess (request coalescing) saved the Baker (the database), but everyone on her spike still waits the whole trip.

Last trick: when the sticker runs out, Della (the cache) doesn't throw the pie away just yet. For a few more minutes she keeps serving it, while one truck fetches a fresh one. Drag across.

It's this morning's pie, a few minutes past its sticker. That's the same trade from chapter two, kept small on purpose.

And at noon, Rosie (your app) gets her slice straight away, and the Baker (the database) bakes one pie.

For grown-ups

stale-while-revalidate
Verified RFC 5861: with Cache-Control: max-age=600, stale-while-revalidate=30 a cache "may continue to be served stale for up to an additional 30 seconds while an asynchronous validation is attempted". After the window, "the next request will block and be handled normally".
Facebook's version
Verified Recently deleted values are kept "for a short time", and "a get request can return a lease token or data that is marked as stale". "Applications that can continue to make forward progress with stale data do not need to wait for the latest value to be fetched from the databases."
Refreshing early
Verified Another fix refreshes a hot key just before it expires, at random, so a stampede becomes very unlikely: XFetch, with beta = 1 and the last rebuild time stored beside the value.

RFC 5861 s3; Nishtala et al., NSDI 2013, s3.2.1; Vattani et al., PVLDB 8(8) 2015, s5

Rosie's lunch, all of it

Now everyone is here. Step through one lunch, start to finish.

Della (the cache) makes it fast. The sticker and the phone call keep it fresh enough. Eddie (the eviction policy) keeps the right pies. Tess (request coalescing) and the old-pie trick keep noon from flattening the farm.

Each of them is there because something broke without them. That's caching: a copy near the people asking, and a handful of rules for when the copy is wrong, full, or missing.
