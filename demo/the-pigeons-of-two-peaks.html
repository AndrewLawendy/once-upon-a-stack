Every Word Arrives

A story for Grandpa

Juno (the sending app) lives on one peak. Grandpa (the receiving app) lives on the other, across a valley far too deep to walk.

She has written him a long story. She wants every word to reach him, in the right order, with nothing missing.

The only way across is the Pigeon Post (IP). It is fast and cheap, but each pigeon flies on its own. Some get lost. Some take the long way round. Nobody promises anything.

That's the real internet. Everything in this book is what the two peaks invent so a long story still arrives whole. Together, those inventions are called TCP.

For grown-ups

What the Pigeon Post promises
Verified IP treats "each internet datagram as an independent entity unrelated to any other internet datagram", and has "no mechanisms to augment end-to-end data reliability, flow control, sequencing" (RFC 791 s1.4, s1.2). Datagrams can be lost, duplicated, reordered or corrupted.
What TCP promises
Verified TCP "must recover from data that is damaged, lost, duplicated, or delivered out of order" (RFC 793 s1.5). Inferred It promises bytes delivered in order to the other machine's kernel, not that the far app has read or used them. The end-to-end argument says the app still has to check what matters to it.

RFC 791 s1.2, s1.4; RFC 793 s1.5; Saltzer, Reed & Clark, End-to-End Arguments in System Design, ACM TOCS 2(4), 1984

One pigeon, one slip

The obvious move: tie the whole story to one pigeon. Juno (the sending app) tries it, and the pigeon drops like a stone.

A pigeon carries one small slip, and no more. Some stretches of the valley are narrower still, and anything too big there is dropped when it can't be split.

So she cuts the story into slips, each small enough to fly. Drag across the picture.

What people see when a slip is too big for the path: the connection opens, small requests work, and big downloads hang until they time out.

For grown-ups

Slips are segments
Verified TCP cuts the byte stream into segments no bigger than the MSS (maximum segment size). "The TCP Maximum Segment Size is the IP maximum datagram size minus forty", and the default is 536 (RFC 879; RFC 9293 s3.7.1: "536 (576 - 40) for IPv4"). Inferred On ordinary 1500-byte Ethernet that is 1460 bytes (1500 minus 20 IP and 20 TCP header bytes).
The narrow stretches
Verified Every link has an MTU. A packet marked "don't fragment" that is too big gets dropped, with an ICMP "fragmentation needed" message sent back (RFC 1191). When that message is blocked, "Classical Path MTU Discovery is subject to protocol failures (connection hangs)" (RFC 4821 s2). Linux can probe around it with net.ipv4.tcp_mtu_probing.

RFC 879; RFC 9293 s3.7.1; RFC 1191; RFC 4821 s2; Linux v6.12 Documentation/networking/ip-sysctl.rst

But each pigeon picks its own way across. One rides a tailwind over the top. One dodges a storm along the valley floor. They land whenever they land.

For grown-ups

Why the order changes
Verified IP has no sequencing (RFC 791 s1.2), and each datagram is handled on its own (s1.4). Inferred Routes change, routers balance load across parallel links, and queues differ, so a later packet can overtake an earlier one.

RFC 791 s1.2, s1.4

A number on every letter

Enter Tally (sequence numbers), an owl with an ink stamp. Before a slip leaves, Tally (sequence numbers) stamps it with one number: the number of its first letter.

Why letters, not slips? If a slip has to be sent again, it might be cut differently. "Letters 201 to 300" always means the same words, however they're cut.

Now Grandpa (the receiving app) can sort. Slip 301 lands early? He keeps it on the side until 201 turns up, then reads straight through.

Where does counting start? Not at 1, it turns out. Hold that thought until chapter two.

For grown-ups

Letters are bytes
Verified "Every octet of data sent over a TCP connection has a sequence number" (RFC 793 s3.3). The 32-bit Sequence Number field holds the number of the segment's first data byte, except on a SYN, where it carries the ISN (RFC 9293 s3.1). Inferred Numbering bytes lets a retransmission be repacked into different segment sizes.
Holding early slips
Verified "Segments with higher beginning sequence numbers SHOULD be held for later processing" (RFC 9293 s3.10.7.4, SHLD-31). Linux keeps them in an out-of-order queue (tcp_data_queue_ofo(), net/ipv4/tcp_input.c).
Story scale
Real numbers start at a hard-to-guess 32-bit value, not 1, and a slip usually holds up to 1460 bytes, not 100 letters.

RFC 793 s3.3; RFC 9293 s3.1, s3.10.7.4; Linux v6.12 net/ipv4/tcp_input.c

The slip that never landed

Imagine if

A storm sweeps the valley, and the pigeon carrying slip 201 goes down.

Grandpa (the receiving app) can see the gap: he has 101 and 301, but nothing in between. Juno (the sending app), on the far peak, has no idea. As far as she knows, every slip is fine.

The person who knows is on the wrong mountain.

What people would see: a document with a paragraph missing, and no error anywhere.

For grown-ups

Why packets vanish
Verified IP drops a datagram whose header checksum fails, or whose time-to-live reaches zero (RFC 791 s1.4, s3.1). Routers also drop packets when their queues are full, which chapter four is about. Often nothing tells the sender: ICMP can report some errors, but a full queue or a bad checksum usually drops the packet without notice.

RFC 791 s1.4, s3.1

  1. Slips 1 and 101 land. Grandpa (the receiving app) sends Echo (acknowledgments) back with one number: the next letter he needs, 201.
  2. Now Juno (the sending app) knows letters 1 to 200 are safe, and can stop worrying about them.
  3. Slip 301 lands, but 201 is still missing. Echo (acknowledgments) says the same thing again: "still need 201". The repeat is a clue, but one repeat could just be a slow pigeon, so nobody acts on it yet.
  4. In the end Juno (the sending app) sends 201 again (knowing when is the next problem). It lands, and one note covers it all: "next I need 401", because 301 was waiting on the side.

A reply flies back

Enter Echo (acknowledgments), a reply pigeon. She carries just one number: the next letter Grandpa needs. Step through it.

That one number says a lot. "Next: 401" means every letter before 401 has arrived. If one reply is lost in the storm, the next one says the same thing and more, so nothing is lost but a moment.

What it can't say is "I have 301 but not 201". For that, the peaks later added a longer note (SACK).

For grown-ups

The ACK number
Verified "If the ACK control bit is set, this field contains the value of the next sequence number the sender of the segment is expecting to receive" (RFC 9293 s3.1). SEG.ACK is the "next sequence number expected by the receiving TCP peer" (s3.4). Inferred So ACK = N means every byte below N arrived. This is a cumulative ACK, and a later ACK repairs a lost earlier one.
How often replies fly
Verified "An ACK SHOULD be generated for at least every second full-sized segment", and any delay "MUST be less than 0.5 seconds" (RFC 9293 s3.8.6.3). Linux delays between 40 ms and 200 ms (TCP_DELACK_MIN, TCP_DELACK_MAX).
Repeats and holes
Verified RFC 5681 treats three duplicate ACKs as a loss signal. Linux starts at 3 (TCP_FASTRETRANS_THRESH) but adapts it to reordering (tcp_reordering) and also uses RACK (tcp_recovery). Selective ACKs (SACK, RFC 2018) can report holes; Linux enables them with net.ipv4.tcp_sack.

RFC 9293 s3.1, s3.4, s3.8.6.3; Linux v6.12 include/net/tcp.h

  1. Slip 201 leaves. Sandy (the retransmission timer) flips over, and the sand starts running.
  2. The storm takes the pigeon. Nothing lands, so Grandpa (the receiving app) sends no new reply.
  3. The sand runs out, and still no word. Waiting longer won't help.
  4. Juno (the sending app) sends slip 201 again, and Sandy (the retransmission timer) flips with twice as much sand, in case the valley is just very busy.
  5. This time it lands, and Echo (acknowledgments) brings back "next I need 301". The sand stops.

An hourglass keeps time

Waiting for Echo (acknowledgments) has a hole in it. If a slip is lost, or the reply is lost, or it was the very last slip, no reply ever comes. Juno (the sending app) would wait forever.

So she gets Sandy (the retransmission timer), an hourglass. When the sand runs out with no reply, the oldest unanswered slip goes again.

Each time it fails, the wait doubles. After about fifteen minutes of silence, she gives up.

What people see when it gives up: Connection timed out.

For grown-ups

The timer rules
Verified RFC 6298 s5: start the timer when sending if it isn't running, turn it off when everything is acknowledged, and on expiry "Retransmit the earliest segment that has not been acknowledged" and "set RTO <- RTO * 2 ('back off the timer')". Linux: icsk_rto = min(icsk_rto << 1, TCP_RTO_MAX) in tcp_retransmit_timer(), with TCP_RTO_MAX of 120 s.
Giving up
Verified net.ipv4.tcp_retries2 defaults to 15: "a hypothetical timeout of 924.6 seconds", which "is a lower bound for the effective timeout". The socket error is ETIMEDOUT (tcp_write_err()). Inferred The message text "Connection timed out" comes from the C library.

RFC 6298 s5; Linux v6.12 net/ipv4/tcp_timer.c, Documentation/networking/ip-sysctl.rst

Wait, how much sand?

One fixed amount fails both ways. Too little, and Sandy (the retransmission timer) runs out while a slow reply is still on its way, so Juno (the sending app) sends copies nobody needed. Too much, and every real loss means a long, idle wait.

So Sandy (the retransmission timer) watches the trips. She keeps a running average of how long a reply takes, and of how much that time wobbles. The sand is set to the average plus four times the wobble.

One rule: never time a slip that was sent twice. When its reply lands, nobody knows which copy it answers.

For grown-ups

The formula
Verified RFC 6298 s2: RTO = SRTT + max(G, K·RTTVAR) with K = 4, smoothing α = 1/8 and β = 1/4; initial RTO 1 second; "if it is less than 1 second, then the RTO SHOULD be rounded up to 1 second"; a maximum is allowed if at least 60 s. Linux's tcp_rtt_estimator() uses the same 1/8 and 1/4 gains, but its minimum RTO is 200 ms (TCP_RTO_MIN = HZ/5).
Karn's rule
Verified "TCP MUST use Karn's algorithm [KP87] for taking RTT samples": "RTT samples MUST NOT be made using segments that were retransmitted" (RFC 6298 s3), unless the timestamp option removes the doubt.
Before this
Verified RFC 793 s3.7 used only a smoothed average times a factor of 1.3 to 2.0. Inferred Ignoring variance caused false timeouts when trip times jumped around, and adding the variance term fixed it.

RFC 6298 s2, s3; RFC 793 s3.7; Linux v6.12 net/ipv4/tcp_input.c, include/net/tcp.h

Every Word Arrives

Twice, and smudged

Resending has a side effect. Sometimes the first slip wasn't lost, only slow, and now Grandpa (the receiving app) has two copies of 201. Easy: he already has letters up to 400, so the extra copy goes in the bin. Tally (sequence numbers) makes that check possible.

Rain can smudge a slip, too. So Juno (the sending app) presses a wax seal on each one: a little sum of every mark on it. If the sum doesn't match, the slip is tossed without a word, and Sandy (the retransmission timer) sees to the rest.

The seal is a cheap one. Now and then a smudge slips past it.

For grown-ups

Duplicates
Verified "Initial tests on arrival are used to discard old duplicates" (RFC 9293 s3.10.7.4). Linux tcp_data_queue(): data ending at or before rcv_nxt is dropped with SKB_DROP_REASON_TCP_OLD_DATA, and an immediate ACK is forced.
The seal is the checksum
Verified "The TCP checksum is never optional" (RFC 9293 s3.1): a 16-bit ones' complement sum over a pseudo-header (both IP addresses, protocol, length), the TCP header and the data. IP's own checksum covers only the IP header (RFC 791 s3.1).
How weak
Verified Stone & Partridge found that "between 1 packet in 1,100 and 1 packet in 32,000 fails the TCP checksum", and that it "will fail to detect errors for roughly 1 in 16 million to 10 billion packets", on their traces. Inferred Because the sum ignores order, swapped 16-bit words go unnoticed. That's why TLS and apps add their own stronger checks.

RFC 9293 s3.1, s3.10.7.4; RFC 1071; Stone & Partridge, When the CRC and TCP Checksum Disagree, SIGCOMM 2000

Every word, in order

The last Echo (acknowledgments) lands beside Juno (the sending app): "next I need 10,001". The story is 10,000 letters long, so that means all of it.

Across the valley, Grandpa (the receiving app) reads it start to finish. He never saw the storm, the copies or the shuffling.

So far: slips small enough to fly. Tally (sequence numbers) puts them in order, Echo (acknowledgments) says what arrived, Sandy (the retransmission timer) resends what didn't, and the seal catches most smudges.

The reply says "next I need 801". What does Grandpa have?Every letter from 1 to 800, for sure. He might also be holding later slips on the side, but the note can't say so.

For grown-ups

What "delivered" means
Verified The receiver reassembles queued segments "into receive buffer and return to user" in order (RFC 9293 s3.10.3). Inferred An ACK means the bytes are in the far kernel's buffer, not that the far app has read them.

RFC 9293 s3.10.3

Yesterday's slip

Imagine if

Numbering works. But where does the counting start? The obvious answer is "at 1, every time".

Yesterday, Juno (the sending app) sent a story about a sick cat. Slip 201 got stuck in a fog bank. Today she starts a new story about a dragon, also counting from 1.

Then yesterday's slip drifts out of the fog and lands. It says 201. Grandpa (the receiving app) has no way to tell it's old, so the cat ends up in the dragon story. Drag across.

So Juno (the sending app) wants something new: a fresh starting number each time, one Grandpa knows and agrees to.

For grown-ups

Old incarnations
Verified A connection is named only by its two addresses and two ports, and "Connections can be reused". The ISN choice must "prevent segments from one incarnation of a connection from being used while the same sequence numbers may still be present in the network from an earlier incarnation" (RFC 9293 s3.4.1). Inferred A fixed start, like 0 or 1, gives every incarnation the same numbers, so a delayed old segment fits right in.
How long slips can drift
Verified TCP assumes a Maximum Segment Lifetime: "the MSL is taken to be 2 minutes" (RFC 9293 s3.4.2). The fog in the story is days, not minutes.
Why not just remember the last number?
Verified It must work "even if a TCP endpoint loses all knowledge of the sequence numbers it has been using", such as after a reboot (RFC 9293 s3.4.1).

RFC 9293 s3.4.1, s3.4.2; RFC 793 s3.3

  1. Juno (the sending app) picks a fresh starting number and knocks: "I'll count from 4,812."
  2. Grandpa (the receiving app) knocks back with two things at once: "I heard 4,812", and his own starting number for his replies.
  3. Juno (the sending app) knocks a third time: "I heard yours."
  4. Now each side has heard the other's number and knows it was heard. The story can start.

Who Goes There?

Three knocks

Before any slip of the story flies, the two peaks agree on their numbers. It takes three knocks. Step through them.

Both sides pick a number, because the talk runs both ways: Grandpa's replies are counted too.

If nobody is home on Grandpa's peak, the door slams straight back.

What people see: Connection refused at once if nothing is listening, or a spinner and then Connection timed out after about two minutes if no knock comes back at all.

For grown-ups

The handshake
Verified SYN carries the client's initial sequence number; SYN-ACK acknowledges it (ACK = ISN+1) and carries the server's; the final ACK acknowledges the server's. "Because steps 2 and 3 can be combined in a single message this is called the three-way (or three message) handshake" (RFC 9293 s3.4.1, s3.5 Fig 6). The SYN uses up one sequence number.
Doors are ports
Verified With no listener, the host answers a SYN with RST (RFC 9293 s3.10.7.1), and Linux returns ECONNREFUSED (tcp_reset()). With no answer at all, Linux v6.12 retries the SYN (tcp_syn_retries 6 plus tcp_syn_linear_timeouts 4) and gives up after about 131 s with ETIMEDOUT.
Who thought of it
Verified RFC 9293 points to Dalal and Sunshine (1978) for discussion of the three-way handshake and clock-driven ISNs. Unknown Tomlinson's 1974-75 sequence-number note may come first; not read.

RFC 9293 s3.4.1, s3.5, s3.10.7.1; Linux v6.12 net/ipv4/tcp_input.c, tcp_timer.c, ip-sysctl.rst

  1. A first knock from yesterday drifts out of the fog. Grandpa (the receiving app) can't tell its age.
  2. He knocks back, as usual, and waits.
  3. Juno (the sending app) never asked for this. Her answer is "not me", and the half-open talk is cancelled.
  4. With only two knocks, Grandpa would already have been talking to a ghost. The third knock is the proof that someone real is there.

Who Goes There?

Wait, why not two?

Two knocks feel like enough: "I'm starting here", "OK". Step through what goes wrong.

Old knocks drift around too. A first knock can't prove it's fresh, because the two peaks don't share a clock. So Grandpa (the receiving app) asks the knocker to prove it, by repeating his brand-new number back.

Only someone who really sent today's knock, and really heard his reply, can do that.

For grown-ups

The reason, in the RFC's words
Verified "The principal reason for the three-way handshake is to prevent old duplicate connection initiations from causing confusion" (RFC 9293 s3.5). And: "A 3WHS is necessary because sequence numbers are not tied to a global clock in the network ... The receiver of the first SYN has no way of knowing whether the segment was an old one or not ... and so it must ask the sender to verify this SYN" (s3.4.1).
The scene
Verified RFC 9293 Figure 8: an old SYN (SEQ=90) arrives first, the server sends SYN-ACK (SEQ=300, ACK=91), the client sees the wrong ACK and sends RST, and the server goes back to LISTEN. Inferred Without the third message, the server would have counted the connection as open.

RFC 9293 s3.4.1, s3.5, Figure 8

Who Goes There?

The masked fox

This really happened: 25 December 1994

Picking numbers from a clock looked safe. Then the Prankster (a spoofing attacker) noticed the clock was predictable.

He can't see Grandpa (the receiving app)'s replies, because they fly to the real Juno (the sending app). But if he can guess Grandpa's number, he can send all three knocks himself, under her name, and Grandpa trusts him.

On Christmas Day 1994, someone did exactly this to Tsutomu Shimomura's computers. Each new number was 128,000 more than the last, and the forger slipped in a command to open the door for good.

The fix: starting numbers mixed with a secret, so no one outside can work out the next one.

For grown-ups

The idea, 1985
Verified Morris described it against 4.2BSD, whose ISN "is incremented by 128 each second and by 64 after each connection": "one need only create a real connection, look in the kernel for the sequence number received, and add 64 to it." The forger also gags the trusted host by filling its queue of pending connections.
Christmas 1994
Verified As reported by Rik Farrow (UnixWorld, 1995): probes saw ISNs rise by 128,000 each time; thirty SYNs gagged the trusted server; a forged handshake sent echo + + >> /.rhosts. Farrow calls it an "alleged" attack by Kevin Mitnick. Unknown Shimomura's own post wasn't read, and the attribution isn't settled by these sources.
The fix
Verified RFC 6528: "ISN = M + F(localip, localport, remoteip, remoteport, secretkey)", where M is a timer and "F() MUST NOT be computable from the outside". Linux uses SipHash with a secret key (secure_tcp_seq()). Inferred It stops only blind, off-path guessing: an attacker who can see the traffic isn't stopped by it.

Morris, A Weakness in the 4.2BSD Unix TCP/IP Software, Bell Labs CSTR 117, 1985; Farrow, Sequence Number Attacks, UnixWorld 1995; RFC 1948; RFC 6528 s3; Linux v6.12 net/core/secure_seq.c

Who Goes There?

The notebook fills up

This really happened: September 1996

After the first knock, Grandpa (the receiving app) jots down who knocked and waits for knock three. The notebook has a few pages.

The Prankster (a spoofing attacker) sends thousands of first knocks under made-up names. None ever knocks a third time, so the notebook fills, and Juno (the sending app)'s real knock is turned away. In September 1996 this knocked out the mail service of Panix, an internet provider in New York.

The fix: write nothing down. Grandpa puts what he'd have noted into his reply number itself, with a secret stamp. Drag across.

What Linux logs: Possible SYN flooding on port ... Sending cookies.

For grown-ups

The attack
Verified CERT CA-1996-21 (19 Sep 1996): the pending-connection structure "is of finite size, and it can be made to overflow by intentionally creating too many partially-open connections", which is "easily accomplished with IP spoofing". The code had appeared in Phrack 48 ("Project Neptune"). Bernstein: Panix mail "was shut down by a SYN flood starting on 6 September 1996".
SYN cookies
Verified Bernstein (cr.yp.to/syncookies.html; details worked out with Eric Schenk, 1996): "SYN cookies are particular choices of initial TCP sequence numbers by TCP servers", built from a time counter, an MSS code and a secret function of the addresses and ports. RFC 4987 s3.6: they "allocate no state at all for connections in SYN-RECEIVED". The cost: limited MSS values, and TCP options are lost unless timestamps carry them.
Linux
Verified net.ipv4.tcp_syncookies defaults to 1 (cookies only when a listener's SYN queue overflows). The docs: "syncookies is fallback facility. It MUST NOT be used to help highly loaded servers to stand against legal connection rate."

CERT CA-1996-21; cr.yp.to/syncookies.html; RFC 4987 s2, s3.6; Linux v6.12 net/ipv4/syncookies.c, tcp_input.c, ip-sysctl.rst

Today's story, and only today's

Three knocks, two fresh numbers, both heard. Juno (the sending app) and Grandpa (the receiving app) now share a conversation that a stray from yesterday is very unlikely to slip into, and that no one in the valley can easily guess their way into.

Every slip from now on carries a number that only makes sense in this conversation.

So far: fresh starting numbers, so old slips don't fit. Three knocks, so a ghost can't open a conversation. Secret-mixed numbers, so the Prankster (a spoofing attacker) can't guess. Stamped replies, so his flood can't fill the notebook.

Why can't the Prankster (a spoofing attacker) finish the three knocks?Knock three must repeat Grandpa's starting number, and that number flies to the real sender. He'd have to guess it, and now it's mixed with a secret.

For grown-ups

ESTABLISHED
Verified After the third segment both ends are in the ESTABLISHED state (RFC 9293 s3.5 Fig 6). Data that arrives with the handshake segments "is buffered at the receiver until the connection reaches the ESTABLISHED state".
What it doesn't stop
Verified RFC 6528 targets off-path attackers. Inferred Secret ISNs defeat off-path guessing only. Someone who can watch the traffic sees the numbers. Real protection against that is cryptographic, such as TLS on top.

RFC 9293 s3.5; RFC 6528

  1. Juno (the sending app) sends one slip.
  2. It lands. She has nothing to do but wait.
  3. Echo (acknowledgments) flies all the way back.
  4. Only now does the next slip go. The valley sits empty most of the time.

Faster, Grandpa!

One at a time

It works, every word arrives. But Juno (the sending app) is impatient. The careful way is to send one slip, wait for its reply, then send the next. Step through it.

However fast the pigeons fly, that's one slip per round trip. If a trip takes a tenth of a second, a 1,460-byte slip at a time comes to under 15 kilobytes a second, on a line that could carry hundreds of times more.

Now she wants something new: the story, faster.

For grown-ups

Stop-and-wait
Inferred Throughput = segment size ÷ round-trip time. 1460 B ÷ 0.1 s = 14.6 KB/s, about 0.1% of a 100 Mbit/s link. Real TCP has never worked this way; it's the baseline that shows why a window is needed.
How much must be in flight
Inferred To fill a pipe you need bandwidth × delay bytes in flight: 100 Mbit/s × 100 ms = 1.25 MB, about 856 full segments.

Arithmetic from RFC 9293 s3.7.1 (MSS) and RFC 7323 s1.1 (bandwidth-delay product)

  1. Juno (the sending app) stops waiting and fills the sky. Replies stream back while new slips stream out. The valley is busy, at last.
  2. But Grandpa (the receiving app) reads slowly. Slips land on his desk tray faster than he takes them off.
  3. The tray overflows. Slips spill to the floor and are lost, and Sandy (the retransmission timer) has to resend them. All that speed, wasted.

Faster, Grandpa!

Fill the sky

So Juno (the sending app) keeps many slips in the air at once, without waiting for each reply. Tally (sequence numbers) and Echo (acknowledgments) still keep track of which is which.

That works beautifully, until you look at the other end. Grandpa's tray is only so big, and he reads at his own pace. Step through it.

She needs to know how much room Grandpa has right now.

For grown-ups

The tray is the receive buffer
Inferred Arriving data waits in the receiver's socket buffer until the app calls read(). Verified Linux sizes it with net.ipv4.tcp_rmem (min, default, max; default 131072 bytes, "This value results in initial window of 65535"), and grows it automatically (tcp_moderate_rcvbuf, on by default).
Without a limit
Inferred A sender that ignores the receiver overruns its buffer, so data is dropped and has to be sent again.

Faster, Grandpa!

Room for 3,000 more

The fix is one more number. Every reply Echo (acknowledgments) carries now says two things: the next letter Grandpa (the receiving app) needs, and how much room is left in his tray.

Juno (the sending app) keeps no more than that in the air. When Grandpa reads a chapter and clears space, the next reply says so, and she speeds up again.

The slow reader sets the pace. That's flow control.

For grown-ups

The Window field
Verified Every segment carries a 16-bit Window: "The number of data octets beginning with the one indicated in the acknowledgment field" that the sender of this segment is willing to accept (RFC 9293 s3.1). The sender keeps unacknowledged data within it.
Too small for fast, far links
Verified 16 bits caps the window at 64 KiB (RFC 7323 s1.1). The Window Scale option, sent "only in a <SYN> segment", multiplies it by up to 214, for about 1 GiB (RFC 7323 s2). Inferred Without scaling, 65,535 bytes per 100 ms trip caps a transfer at about 5 Mbit/s.
Advertising sensibly
Verified Receivers "MUST include a SWS avoidance algorithm" so they don't advertise tiny windows one byte at a time (silly window syndrome, RFC 813; RFC 9293 s3.8.6.2.2).

RFC 9293 s3.1, s3.8.6.2; RFC 7323 s1.1, s2; RFC 813

  1. Grandpa (the receiving app) falls behind, and the reply says "room for 0". Juno (the sending app) stops, as she should.
  2. He reads, the tray empties, and he sends a reply: "room for 5,000".
  3. The storm takes that reply. It carried no story, so nobody is timing it, and nobody will resend it.
  4. Now she waits for room, and he waits for slips. Nothing is in the air, so nothing will ever change.
  5. Unless a second, smaller timer nudges her: every so often, send one tiny slip anyway. The answer to it always carries the current room.

Faster, Grandpa!

Room for zero

The room number has its own trap. Step through it.

A reply that only says "there's room again" has nothing in it to resend. If the storm takes it, both peaks wait for each other, politely, for ever.

So while the room is zero, Juno (the sending app) sends a probe now and then: a one-letter slip that asks "room yet?". The gaps between probes grow longer, like the waits of Sandy (the retransmission timer).

What people see: a transfer that stops dead with no error. A packet capture shows TCP ZeroWindow, then TCP ZeroWindowProbe.

For grown-ups

Why it would stall
Verified ACKs that carry no data are not transmitted reliably (RFC 9293 s3.8.4). Inferred So a lost window update leaves no retransmission to repair it, and both ends wait.
The probe
Verified "The sending TCP peer must regularly transmit at least one octet of new data (if available), or retransmit ... even if the send window is zero, in order to 'probe' the window"; the first probe after one RTO, then "increase exponentially the interval between successive probes" (RFC 9293 s3.8.6.1, SHLD-29, SHLD-30). Linux: tcp_probe_timer(). A receiver may keep its window closed indefinitely, and the sender must keep the connection open as long as probes are answered (MAY-8, MUST-37).
Wireshark's names
Verified TCP ZeroWindow, TCP ZeroWindowProbe, TCP ZeroWindowProbeAck, TCP Window Update, TCP Window Full.

RFC 9293 s3.8.4, s3.8.6.1; RFC 1122 s4.2.2.17; Linux v6.12 net/ipv4/tcp_timer.c; Wireshark User's Guide, TCP Analysis

Faster, Grandpa!

As fast as he can read

Now the story flows. Juno (the sending app) keeps the sky full, but never fuller than Grandpa (the receiving app)'s tray. When he pauses, she pauses. When he's ready, his reply says so, and a probe covers it if that reply is lost.

So far: many slips in the air at once (a window). Every reply says how much room is left, so a slow reader is never flooded, and a probe makes sure a closed window can always open again.

A download sits at exactly the same slow speed on a fast, far-away link. Which number might be the limit?The room number. If the window can't grow past 64 KiB (no window scaling), you get at most one window per round trip, however fast the link is.

For grown-ups

Small-slip rules (not in the story)
Verified Nagle's algorithm (RFC 896, 1984) holds back new small segments while earlier data is unacknowledged, against "41 byte packets (one byte of data, 40 bytes of header)". Delayed ACKs (RFC 9293 s3.8.6.3) wait up to 0.5 s to combine replies. In common systems "both the Nagle algorithm and delayed acknowledgments are implemented and enabled by default" (RFC 9293 App. A.3), and an app can disable Nagle (MUST-17). Inferred Together they can add 40-200 ms to small request/response exchanges on Linux; TCP_NODELAY is the usual fix.

RFC 896; RFC 9293 s3.7.4, s3.8.6.3, App. A.3; Linux v6.12 include/net/tcp.h

The Valley Jams

The roost in the middle

Juno (the sending app) now sends as much as Grandpa's tray allows. So does every other village on every other peak.

All their pigeons rest at the same roost tower halfway across, kept by Rook (a router's queue). He has only so many perches. Birds wait their turn, and when every perch is taken, the next one is turned away. It simply falls.

Grandpa's room number says nothing about the roost. Two peaks can agree on a fine speed while the valley between them chokes. So Juno (the sending app) wants something new: to get through even when the whole valley is busy.

For grown-ups

The roost is a router's queue
Verified Routers (then called gateways) buffer packets for a slower outgoing link, and the buffer is finite: "Eventually all available buffers in the switching nodes will be full and packets must be dropped" (RFC 896). Jacobson and Karels saw gateways "drop 10% of the incoming packets because of local buffer overflows". "when a packet arrives to a full buffer, it is dropped" (Gettys & Nichols, 2011).
Two different limits
Inferred The receive window protects the receiver; nothing in it describes the path. Verified Jacobson & Karels s1 give a path example: 10 Mbit/s Ethernet feeding a 56 kbit/s line.

RFC 896; Jacobson & Karels, Congestion Avoidance and Control, SIGCOMM 1988 (rev. Nov 1988) s1; Gettys & Nichols, Bufferbloat, ACM Queue 2011

The Valley Jams

The day the valley stopped

This really happened: October 1986

Here's the trap. A pigeon turned away by Rook (a router's queue) never lands, so every sender's Sandy (the retransmission timer) runs out, and every sender resends. Now there are more pigeons, so more are turned away, so more are resent.

Soon the sky is full of copies of copies, and almost nothing arrives. In October 1986, the line between a lab in Berkeley and the university campus, 400 yards apart, fell from 32,000 bits a second to 40. Drag across.

What people saw: throughput that fell about a thousandfold.

For grown-ups

The numbers
Verified "the data throughput from LBL to UC Berkeley (sites separated by 400 yards and two IMP hops) dropped from 32 Kbps to 40 bps", a "factor-of-thousand drop", "the first of what became a series of 'congestion collapses'" (Jacobson & Karels, Introduction; revised 1988 copy).
Named in 1984
Verified Nagle, RFC 896 (6 Jan 1984): hosts "will begin to introduce more and more copies of the same datagrams into the net ... Hosts are sending each packet several times, and eventually some copy of each packet arrives at its destination. This is congestion collapse." The phrase is Nagle's coinage (Jacobson & Karels, footnote 5).

Jacobson & Karels 1988, Introduction; RFC 896

  1. A new conversation. Wren (congestion control) lets only a few slips go, because she knows nothing about the valley yet.
  2. Their replies come back safely, so each reply lets one more slip go. Twice as many are in the air now.
  3. Still fine. Twice again. Growth like this finds the valley's limit in a handful of round trips.
  4. Then a slip goes missing at Rook (a router's queue)'s full roost. That's the signal: the valley is full. Wren (congestion control) stops climbing.

The Valley Jams

A scout who listens

Resending harder made the jam worse. What Juno (the sending app) needs is someone who treats a lost pigeon as a message: the valley is full, ease off.

Enter Wren (congestion control), a scout. She keeps her own count of how many slips the valley can take. She sends no more than the smaller of two numbers: the scout's count, and Grandpa's room.

She starts small and grows fast. Step through it. Her start is called "slow start", which is funny, because it doubles every round trip.

For grown-ups

The congestion window
Verified Jacobson and Karels added "a congestion window, cwnd, to the per-connection state": "When starting or restarting after a loss, set cwnd to one packet. On each ack for new data, increase cwnd by one packet. When sending, send the minimum of the receiver's advertised window and cwnd." RFC 5681 s3.1: "The minimum of cwnd and rwnd governs data transmission." cwnd lives only in the sender and is never sent.
How small is "a few"
Verified 1988: one packet. RFC 5681: up to 4 segments. RFC 6928 (2013, Experimental): 10 segments. Linux: TCP_INIT_CWND 10 ("as per rfc6928"). Inferred So the first round trip carries about 14.6 KB, and bigger pages need more trips.
The name
Verified First called "soft-start", renamed "slow-start" by John Nagle in March 1987 (Jacobson & Karels, footnote 2).

Jacobson & Karels 1988 s1, s2; RFC 5681 s3.1; RFC 6928; Linux v6.12 include/net/tcp.h, net/ipv4/tcp_cong.c

  1. Past the first loss, Wren (congestion control) stops doubling. She adds just one slip per round trip, creeping up to see if there's room.
  2. Three replies in a row say "still need 201". The others got through, so one slip was lost, and the valley is only a little too full. Resend 201 at once, and cut the count in half.
  3. Creep up again, one slip per trip.
  4. This time nothing comes back at all, and Sandy (the retransmission timer) runs out. That's bad news: start again from one slip and climb.

The Valley Jams

Up slowly, down fast

Doubling forever would overshoot, and waiting for Sandy (the retransmission timer) after every loss would waste a whole round of sand. So Wren (congestion control) has a second gear. Step through it.

Up slowly, down fast. A jam grows fast, so she backs off fast. Room appears slowly, so she probes slowly.

If two villages both play this way, the one sending more loses more when it halves. Over time, they drift towards sharing the valley.

For grown-ups

AIMD
Verified Jacobson and Karels: "On any timeout, set cwnd to half the current window size"; "On each ack for new data, increase cwnd by 1/cwnd (this is the additive increase)". A symmetric multiplicative increase "will oscillate wildly and, on the average, deliver poor throughput". RFC 5681: ssthresh = max(FlightSize/2, 2·SMSS); in congestion avoidance cwnd grows about one SMSS per RTT.
Two kinds of loss
Verified "The fast retransmit algorithm uses the arrival of 3 duplicate ACKs" as a loss signal; fast recovery then continues at the reduced window (RFC 5681 s3.2; NewReno RFC 6582 handles several losses per window). On a retransmission timeout, "cwnd MUST be set to no more than the loss window, LW, which equals 1 full-sized segment" (s3.1).
Fairness
Inferred Chiu and Jain (1989) showed AIMD moves competing flows towards equal shares. That paper wasn't read for this book, and the routers themselves enforce nothing (Jacobson & Karels s4).

Jacobson & Karels 1988 s3, s4; RFC 5681 s3.1, s3.2; RFC 6582; Linux v6.12 net/ipv4/tcp_cong.c

The Valley Jams

Wait, which scout?

Wren (congestion control) has cousins, and peaks pick one. Two matter today.

CUBIC is the usual one on Linux, Windows and Apple machines. After a loss she cuts to 70%, not half. Then she climbs on a curve set by the clock: quickly at first, carefully near the last peak, then quickly again. On a long valley, she doesn't fall behind.

BBR doesn't wait for a lost pigeon at all. She measures how fast replies really return and the shortest trip she has seen, and sends at that pace.

Why bother? Some roosts have grown huge. Pigeons wait there for ages before any are turned away, so waiting for a loss means waiting in a long, slow queue.

For grown-ups

CUBIC
Verified RFC 9438: "uses a cubic function instead of a linear congestion window increase function"; beta_cubic "SHOULD be set to 0.7"; growth depends on time since the last reduction; "CUBIC has been adopted as the default TCP congestion control algorithm by the Linux, Windows, and Apple stacks." Linux v6.12 Kconfig: default "cubic"; tcp_cubic.c beta = 717/1024. Inferred Default in Linux since 2.6.19 (2006), from secondary sources.
BBR
Verified Linux tcp_bbr.c: "bottleneck_bandwidth = windowed_max(delivered / elapsed, 10 round trips); min_rtt = windowed_min(rtt, 10 seconds)". It isn't the default. Inferred The ACM Queue article (Cardwell et al., 2016) couldn't be read for this book.
Huge roosts
Verified Bufferbloat: "the existence of excessively large and frequently full buffers inside the network", which "damage or defeat the fundamental congestion-avoidance algorithms" (Gettys & Nichols, ACM Queue, Dec 2011).
Choosing
Verified net.ipv4.tcp_congestion_control; per socket with TCP_CONGESTION.

RFC 9438 s4; Linux v6.12 net/ipv4/Kconfig, tcp_cubic.c, tcp_bbr.c, ip-sysctl.rst; Gettys & Nichols 2011

The Valley Jams

A valley that keeps moving

Every village now has its own Wren (congestion control). When Rook (a router's queue) turns a pigeon away, the senders ease off together, and the roost drains instead of overflowing.

Juno (the sending app)'s story gets through on a busy day: not as fast as on an empty one, but steadily. And nobody's resending makes the jam worse.

So far: two limits on every send. Grandpa's room protects Grandpa. Wren (congestion control)'s count protects the valley. Grow slowly, halve on a loss, start over if Sandy (the retransmission timer) runs out.

Why do the first few kilobytes of a new connection feel slower than the rest?A new conversation starts with only about 10 slips in the air, and doubles each round trip. The full speed takes a few trips to reach.

For grown-ups

Why it works
Verified "The system will stabilize only if the traffic sources throttle back at least as quickly as the queues are growing" (Jacobson & Karels s3). Packet drops remain the congestion signal for loss-based algorithms (s4).
Cost of a fresh start
Verified Reaching window W takes about R·log2W, where R is the round-trip time (Jacobson & Karels s1). RFC 6928's larger initial window saves "up to 4 round trips" for transfers over 4 KB, compared with an initial window of 3 segments (s5.1).

Jacobson & Karels 1988 s1, s3, s4; RFC 6928 s5.1

Saying Goodbye

Just stop?

Imagine if

The story is home. Now Juno (the sending app) wants to finish properly. Both peaks are still keeping a counter, a tray and an hourglass for this conversation, and they can't keep them forever.

The easy way is to stop sending and walk off. But on the other peak, silence looks exactly like a storm. Grandpa (the receiving app) can't tell "that was the end" from "the end got lost". So he waits, tray out, with nothing to tell him why. Drag across.

What people see: an app stuck "loading" on a reply that will never come, or a server slowly running out of connections.

For grown-ups

An explicit end
Verified The FIN flag means "No more data from sender" (RFC 9293 s3.1). Inferred Without it, an idle connection is indistinguishable from a stalled one, and its state (the TCB: buffers, timers, sequence numbers) is held until something times out. Verified Keepalives "MUST default to off" (RFC 9293 s3.8.4, MUST-25). Inferred So an idle connection never times out on its own.

RFC 9293 s3.1, s3.6

  1. Juno (the sending app) sends "that's all from me". It's numbered like a letter, so it can't overtake the end of the story.
  2. Grandpa (the receiving app) confirms it, the way he'd confirm any letter. Her side is closed, but she keeps listening.
  3. He isn't finished: he still has something to say. When he's done, he sends his own "that's all from me".
  4. She confirms. Both directions are closed, each by the side that was talking.

Saying Goodbye

Two goodbyes

So Juno (the sending app) says it out loud: "that's all from me". But one goodbye can't close both ways. She's finished talking, and Grandpa (the receiving app) may not be.

So each side says its own goodbye, and each goodbye gets its own reply. Step through it.

What a stuck goodbye looks like: lots of connections in CLOSE-WAIT means the other side said goodbye and your app never answered. It usually means the app hasn't closed its end: often a bug, a connection opened and never closed.

For grown-ups

FIN takes a number
Verified "the FIN is considered to occur after the last actual data octet in a segment in which it occurs", and it is acknowledged like data (RFC 9293 s3.4).
Half-close
Verified CLOSE means "I have no more to send" but does not mean "I will not receive any more" (RFC 9293 s3.9.1.4); "a host is permitted to continue sending data in the open direction on a half-closed connection" (s3.6.1). shutdown(fd, SHUT_WR) sends a FIN and keeps the socket readable (Linux tcp_shutdown()).
States along the way
Verified The closer goes ESTABLISHED → FIN-WAIT-1 → FIN-WAIT-2 → TIME-WAIT; the other side ESTABLISHED → CLOSE-WAIT → LAST-ACK → CLOSED (RFC 9293 s3.3.2, s3.6). Inferred CLOSE-WAIT has no timer: it ends only when the local app calls close().

RFC 9293 s3.3.2, s3.4, s3.6, s3.6.1, s3.9.1.4; Linux v6.12 net/ipv4/tcp.c

  1. Juno (the sending app)'s final "heard, goodbye" is caught in a storm.
  2. Grandpa (the receiving app) never got it, so his own Sandy (the retransmission timer) runs out and he sends his goodbye again.
  3. If she had already left, his goodbye would get a slammed door instead of a polite answer. She's still there, so she answers, and he closes cleanly.
  4. Meanwhile, any slips still drifting from this conversation fall out of the sky before a new one can reuse the same doors.

Saying Goodbye

Wait a little, with the lantern on

The last message, "heard, goodbye", gets no reply. If it's lost, nobody would know. So Juno (the sending app), who said goodbye first, doesn't leave at once. She sits on her peak a while, lantern lit, until it burns down. Step through why.

That wait is TIME-WAIT: on Linux, one minute.

What people see: thousands of TIME-WAIT lines in ss -tan on a busy machine. That's normal, and it's the side that closed first. It usually only matters for a machine opening huge numbers of short connections to the same address and port, which can run out of ports.

For grown-ups

Two reasons
Verified TIME-WAIT exists "to be sure the remote TCP peer received the acknowledgment of its connection termination request" and "to avoid new connections being impacted by delayed segments from previous connections" (RFC 9293 s3.3.2). "When a connection is closed actively, it MUST linger in the TIME-WAIT state for a time 2xMSL" (s3.6.1, MUST-13). A retransmitted FIN restarts the 2 MSL wait (s3.10.7.4).
How long
Verified MSL "is taken to be 2 minutes" (RFC 9293 s3.4.2), so 4 minutes by the RFC. Linux uses a fixed TCP_TIMEWAIT_LEN (60*HZ), 60 seconds. tcp_fin_timeout (60 s) is a different timer, for orphaned FIN-WAIT-2 sockets.
A shortcut that broke
Verified tcp_tw_recycle was removed (commit 4396e46187ca; man tcp(7): "Linux 2.4 to 4.11") because it "was already broken for connections behind NAT". tcp_tw_reuse defaults to 2 (loopback only) in v6.12.

RFC 9293 s3.3.2, s3.4.2, s3.6.1, s3.10.7.4; Linux v6.12 include/net/tcp.h, ip-sysctl.rst; commit 4396e46187ca

Saying Goodbye

The slammed door

There is a rude way out: slam the door. One note that says "reset, this conversation is over", with no reply and no lantern. It's for when politeness is impossible: nobody home at that door, a peak that crashed and forgot everything, or an app that quits with unread slips on its tray. Anything still in the air is thrown away.

And the Prankster (a spoofing attacker) is back, in a new form: one sitting right on the path, who can read the numbers instead of guessing them. A slam note needs only the right doors and a believable number. In 2007 an internet provider, Comcast, was found sending forged slams to both ends of its customers' file-sharing connections, each pretending to be from the other side. Juno (the sending app) would never have known.

What people see: Connection reset by peer.

For grown-ups

When RST is sent
Verified "reset (RST) is sent whenever a segment arrives that apparently is not intended for the current connection" (RFC 9293 s3.5.2). Linux __tcp_close() sends RST instead of FIN when unread data is queued ("As outlined in RFC 2525, section 2.17, we send a RST here because data was lost"), or with SO_LINGER set to zero. An RST is checked only by its sequence number (s3.5.3) and is never acknowledged. Inferred The error text "Connection reset by peer" is the C library's message for ECONNRESET.
Comcast, 2007
Verified EFF, "Packet Forgery By ISPs: A Report on the Comcast Affair" (28 Nov 2007): forged RST packets that each side saw as coming from the other; "The only likely explanation of these observations is that Comcast was forging and injecting the RST packets". Inferred From secondary summaries: the FCC ruled against Comcast in Order 08-183 (Aug 2008), later vacated by a court in 2010. An on-path injector can see the sequence numbers, so secret ISNs don't help here.

RFC 9293 s3.5.2, s3.5.3; Linux v6.12 net/ipv4/tcp.c; EFF 2007; FCC 08-183

Saying Goodbye

Goodnight, both ways

Two goodbyes, two replies, a lantern left on for a while in case the last word went astray. Then Juno (the sending app) puts the lantern out.

Both peaks know the story is finished. Counters, trays and hourglasses are put away, and no stray slip will wander into tomorrow's story.

So far: each side says its own goodbye, numbered like a letter. Whoever says it first lingers in TIME-WAIT. A reset is the slammed door: quick, final, and easy to forge.

A server has 5,000 connections in CLOSE-WAIT. Where do you look?At the server's own app. The other side said goodbye; the app hasn't closed its end, usually a bug or a stuck thread.

For grown-ups

The end state
Verified "If the time-wait timeout expires on a connection, delete the TCB, enter the CLOSED state" (RFC 9293 s3.10.8). CLOSED "represents no connection state at all" (s3.3.2).

RFC 9293 s3.3.2, s3.10.8

  1. Juno (the sending app) knocks three times. Fresh numbers both ways, mixed with a secret, so the Prankster (a spoofing attacker) can't guess them and his flood of fake knocks can't fill a notebook.
  2. The story goes in slips. Tally (sequence numbers) stamps every slip with its first letter's number, and Wren (congestion control) starts small and doubles each round trip, but never past Grandpa's room.
  3. Echo (acknowledgments) flies back with "next I need" and "room for". Sandy (the retransmission timer) times each round trip.
  4. Rook (a router's queue)'s roost is full, and a pigeon falls. Repeated replies give it away: resend it, and Wren (congestion control) halves her count. The valley keeps moving.
  5. The last letter lands. Two goodbyes, two replies, and a while with the lantern on. Grandpa (the receiving app) reads every word, in order.

The whole trip, once more

Now everyone is here. Step through one story, start to finish.

Tally (sequence numbers), Echo (acknowledgments) and Sandy (the retransmission timer) make it reliable. The three knocks make it a conversation. Grandpa's room keeps it kind to the reader. Wren (congestion control) keeps it kind to the valley, where Rook (a router's queue) has only so many perches. Two goodbyes make it finish.

Every one of them is there because something broke without them. That's TCP: a reliable conversation built out of unreliable pigeons.
